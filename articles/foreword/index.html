Foreword

The Himalaya have often been described as a great natural barrier separating civilizations and states. History presents a more nuanced picture.

Shyam Saran 1 August 2026 · 4 min read
Share

The Himalaya have often been described as a great natural barrier separating civilizations and states. History presents a more nuanced picture. For centuries, these mountains connected people as much as they divided them. Ideas, religious traditions, trade and culture found their way across high mountain passes, creating enduring links between the Indian subcontinent, the Tibetan Plateau, and the wider Himalayan region. The Himalaya and the vast plateau beyond have shaped Asia's history in profound ways, although this interconnected past is not always fully appreciated.

Today, the region is once again undergoing significant change. Strategic competition, environmental pressures, water security, climate change, and rapid technological advances are reshaping the political and ecological landscape of Asia. Developments on the Tibetan Plateau increasingly have consequences that extend far beyond its immediate neighbourhood, influencing the security, environmental stability, and economic well-being of countries across the region.

During my years in diplomacy, our northern frontiers were viewed primarily through the lens of boundary negotiations and border management. Those issues remain important and will continue to require careful attention. But they represent only one dimension of a much larger Himalayan reality. Questions of security today are closely intertwined with ecology, water resources, infrastructure, local communities, economic development, and the wider geopolitical changes taking place across Asia. A fuller understanding of these interconnections is no longer simply an academic pursuit, it is an essential element of informed public policy.

It is therefore a pleasure for me as a Trustee of the Foundation for Non-violent Alternatives, to welcome the publication of Trans Himalaya. The journal reflects the Foundation's longstanding commitment to encouraging rigorous scholarship, fostering informed dialogue, and promoting a deeper understanding of Tibet and the wider Himalayan and Trans Himalayan region.

I am pleased to see this commitment take shape in a publication that I hope will become a valuable resource for scholars, policymakers, and all those interested in this important part of Asia.

One of the strengths of Trans Himalaya lies in its interdisciplinary approach. History, strategy, international relations, ecology, religion, culture, and economics are often studied independently of one another. Yet, the Himalayan region does not lend itself to such neat divisions. Geography influences politics, ecology shapes security, culture and history continue to inform contemporary diplomacy. A meaningful understanding of the region requires these subjects to be viewed together rather than in isolation.

It is fitting that the inaugural issue is devoted to Tibet. Few places illustrate more clearly the relationship between geography, history, and geopolitics. Tibet occupies a distinctive place in Asia. It is the source of many of the continent's great rivers, home to an extraordinary civilisational tradition, and a region whose strategic significance has grown steadily in recent decades. No serious understanding of the Trans Himalayan region can be complete without an appreciation of Tibet's historical, cultural, ecological and strategic importance.

Equally important is the recognition that scholarship on the Himalaya should not remain confined to academic institutions alone. It has much to contribute to informed public debate and to better policymaking. Publications that encourage careful research, thoughtful discussion and informed analysis perform a valuable public service, particularly at a time when commentary on the region is often shaped by immediacy rather than perspective.

Future editions of Trans Himalaya will broaden this conversation by exploring other parts of Asia's upland world and the many historical, cultural, and strategic linkages that bind them together. I hope the journal will provide a forum where scholars, practitioners, and policymakers can engage with one another and where academic research can contribute more directly to public understanding and policy discourse.

The publication of this inaugural issue reflects the efforts of many individuals. I congratulate Professor Srikanth Kondapalli, Editor, the members of the Editorial Board, the Governing Council, and my fellow Trustees of the Foundation for Non-violent Alternatives for bringing this ambitious project to fruition. I would also like to acknowledge the vision and perseverance of Rebon Banerjee Dhar, whose commitment has been central to transforming the idea of Trans Himalaya into a reality. It is particularly encouraging to see the younger generation of scholars—both on the Editorial Board and among the contributors to this inaugural issue—bringing fresh perspectives while upholding the highest standards of scholarship.

There is no shortage of commentary on the Himalayan region today. What is often lacking is careful analysis grounded in historical understanding, rigorous research, and informed debate. If Trans Himalaya succeeds in fostering such scholarship and encouraging thoughtful engagement with one of Asia's most consequential regions, it will make a meaningful contribution to both academic discourse and public policy.

The Himalaya have shaped Asia's past in profound ways and will continue to influence its future. Understanding this remarkable region in all its complexity is therefore not merely an academic undertaking but an investment in informed statecraft.

I wish Trans Himalaya every success and hope that, in the years ahead, it will establish itself as a respected forum for scholarship, dialogue ,and informed public discourse.

Ambassador Shyam Saran

Former Foreign Secretary of India

Former Chairman, National Security Advisory Board

Trustee, Foundation for Non-violent Alternatives

16.7.2026
