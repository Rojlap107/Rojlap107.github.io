Held at the Bureau of His Holiness the Dalai Lama, New Delhi

Host: Tenzing Dhamdul, Senior Researcher, FNVA

Tashi Delek, Greetings. It's both an honor and privilege to welcome Your Excellency Sikyong Penpa Tsering la of the Central Tibetan Administration for an exclusive inaugural edition interview for the Trans-Himalaya Journal. As the democratically elected political leader of the Tibetan people in exile, His Excellency has been at the forefront of efforts to preserve the Tibetan identity, advocate for the rights and aspirations of the Tibetan people and strengthen international awareness of the Tibet cause. In this conversation, we explore with His Excellency perspectives on the future of Tibet, the role of the Central Tibetan Administration, the preservation of the Tibetan heritage and the broader significance of the Tibet-China conflict in our contemporary world.

Thank you for having me. And I wish Trans-Himalaya Journal very good success, good readership and good followers.

First and foremost, congratulations on your re-election. As you begin your second term with a decisive mandate, what are the core strategic pillars of your administration for the next five years? And additionally, your Kashag (Cabinet) has already been formed with Six Kalons (Ministers). How does this specific leadership structure serve your governance in the next five years?

On the political front, we have three main responsibilities: resolving the Sino-Tibet conflict, delivering social services to Tibetan communities in exile, particularly in India and Nepal, and ensuring an efficient administration to support both. Right now, if you look at Chinese policies, especially in Tibet and other nationality regions, there appears to be very little space for dialogue. However, back channels still exist, and it is important that they remain open. We know that any lasting solution to the Sino-Tibet conflict can only come from China. We follow the Middle Way Approach, which is based on non-violence and a mutually beneficial negotiated solution. Although Tibet was historically independent, His Holiness is not seeking independence. He is seeking meaningful autonomy. Even though there are autonomous regions and prefectures in name, there is no genuine autonomy in practice. Until there is leadership in China willing to seriously engage with this proposal, we must continue building international support for the Middle Way Approach. The United States has already adopted the Resolve Tibet Act, and we hope other like-minded countries will take similar positions. Another issue that deeply concerns the Chinese government is the future reincarnation of the 14th Dalai Lama. In many ways, they seem more concerned about the future 15th Dalai Lama than the living 14th. They believe that if they can control the recognition of the next Dalai Lama, they can control Tibet. That is why we are working to build awareness and support among Tibetans, the wider Buddhist world, and the international community. So far, we have received support from around 38 democratic countries. Alongside these international efforts, we also remain focused on the welfare, rehabilitation, and long-term well-being of Tibetan refugee communities in exile.

We face several challenges. One is the dispersal of young people from rural Tibetan communities in India. Over the last 65 years, our settlements across India have played a crucial role in preserving our language, culture, religion, arts, and crafts in exile. We need to keep these communities vibrant and sustainable. A second challenge concerns more than 1,800 Tibetan families in India and Nepal who do not have homes of their own. We are encouraging them to move to larger settlements, not only to address their housing needs but also to strengthen these communities for the long term. Today, Tibetans are spread across nearly 30 countries, and our communities are becoming less compact than they once were. Then, of course, there are ongoing responsibilities in health, education, religion, and culture. These are essential not only for the welfare of Tibetans in exile but also for preserving our identity and heritage. To do all of this, we need an efficient administration. Over the last few years, we have been working to strengthen both the institutional and individual capacities of the Central Tibetan Administration, and I would say we are about halfway there. Looking ahead, a lot of our focus will be on data, technology, and human capacity. The world is entering the age of artificial intelligence, and we do not want to fall behind. We are already moving towards AI-assisted systems for administration, budgeting, reporting, and project tracking. These tools should help improve efficiency, accountability, and transparency. In my view, transparency remains the best way to counter disinformation. These are some of the key areas we need to focus on over the next five years.

Sikyong la, you mentioned transparency and accountability. I think this is something that the Central Tibetan Administration stands for. And going on what you mentioned about the Tibet Autonomous Region. We have seen in the past few years the engagements that China has been carrying out, it was only last year that President Xi Jinping visited the Tibet Autonomous Region to celebrate its 60th year anniversary. He continues to remain a dominant figure when it comes to reshaping, administrating and governing the Tibet Autonomous Region and its subsequent Tibet Autonomous Prefectures. Based on your administration's analysis, what is your current reading of President Xi Jinping's long-term vision on Tibet? And do you see any strategic entry points of flexibility on Tibet to be engaged upon?

That is why I said there appears to be very little space for dialogue if you look at the policies being implemented today. The thinking behind President Xi Jinping's administration is that if there are no distinct national identities such as Tibetans, Uyghurs, or Mongols, then there will be no nationality issues. Since these peoples cannot simply be eliminated, the approach is assimilation—to gradually turn them into Chinese. Many of the campaigns and programmes of recent years reflect this objective. What is particularly concerning is that these policies are now being given the force of law. One example is the Ethnic Unity and Progress Law, adopted earlier this year. It provides a legal framework to enforce assimilation policies and includes provisions that can even be applied beyond China's borders against those seen as opposing its objectives.

Whenever it comes to Communist Party policies and programmes, they are often given very appealing names. The Ethnic Unity and Progress Law is one example. It sounds positive, but in reality it is about assimilation and the gradual erosion of distinct ethnic identities. Language is a good example. Tibetan children are increasingly taught in Mandarin rather than in their own language. Yet Tibetan is one of the world's oldest living languages. Recently, a Tibetan monk completed a Tibetan-to-Tibetan dictionary in 223 volumes, which shows the richness and depth of the language. When a language is weakened, culture, religion, and an entire way of life are also put at risk. These are the foundations of a people's identity. Then there is the issue of Tibet's environment. The consequences will not be limited to Tibetans alone. If Tibet's environment is damaged, future generations in China will also be affected. Protecting Tibet's environment is therefore not only in the interest of Tibetans, but in the interest of China as well.

Xi Jinping visited Tibet last year, but there were several other important developments as well. For those unfamiliar with the issue, the 10th Panchen Lama passed away in 1989 under circumstances many Tibetans consider suspicious. His reincarnation was identified in 1995 and recognised by His Holiness the Dalai Lama. On the very day that recognition was announced, the boy and his family disappeared. Since then, we have not known whether the Panchen Lama recognised by His Holiness is still alive. In the meantime, the Chinese government appointed its own Panchen Lama and has been promoting him as part of its effort to influence the future recognition of the 15th Dalai Lama. So these developments are very much connected. More broadly, I often say that China today stands on one pillar—Xi Jinping. He controls the Party, the government, and the military. Over the last several years, we have also seen more power shifting from state institutions to the Communist Party itself, with Party commissions playing an increasingly decisive role. At the same time, there has been growing militarisation, tighter security measures, and a greater atmosphere of suspicion, reflected in laws such as the Anti-Espionage Law. China remains a very opaque system, but if you watch developments closely, certain patterns become visible. The repeated purges and removals of senior officials, both in political circles and within the military, suggest that significant tensions exist within the system. The situation in the military is particularly striking, with a number of senior figures investigated, removed, or disappearing from public view. That raises important questions about the state of China's top leadership and institutions today.

The economy is also facing challenges. Trade and commerce are slowing in some sectors, while China's energy demands remain enormous. Even with large energy reserves and major investments in renewables, sustaining an industrial base that produces goods accounting for roughly 33 per cent of global manufacturing output requires vast amounts of energy. At the same time, there are concerns about weak domestic consumption and problems in the housing sector. A number of pressures are building within the economy, and it is difficult to predict how they may unfold. There is also the issue of youth unemployment. At one point, the Chinese government stopped releasing certain unemployment figures, which itself reflected the sensitivity of the issue. Many young people are struggling to find suitable jobs, and we are now seeing reforms to the hukou system for rural migrants living and working in cities. Let us see how these changes affect Chinese society. If these challenges are not addressed, they could develop into much larger problems. That is why we need to think about different scenarios for China and consider what challenges, as well as opportunities, they might present for Tibet and the Tibetan people.

When you mention the level of authority that Xi Jinping has, it very much contrasts to his Holiness the Dalai Lama, where in 2011 he devolved his political authority to the Central Tibetan Administration. And talking on his reincarnation, recently your very remarks on the Hindustan times regarding the succession and reincarnation of His Holiness, drew a sharp response from the Chinese Embassy here in New Delhi who asserted their position. What is your message to China's claim over such a sacred spiritual lineage for Tibetans and followers of Buddhism for that matter? And how is the Central Tibetan Administration positioning itself globally on this issue? And there is some talk of whether President Xi Jinping would visit India this September for the BRIC Summit. In this context is there a possibility of engagements with the very leader who holds all the powers here when it comes to China?

I would say that we are not looking for one, and I do not think there can be one. Religion and spirituality are matters of faith. They cannot be imposed by someone else telling you what religion to follow or what religion not to follow. It becomes even more difficult to accept when such directives come from a state that officially does not believe in religion. For Tibetans, this only deepens wounds that already exist. Tibetan society has been rooted in Buddhist faith and values for more than 1,300 years. That faith is deeply woven into our culture, identity, and daily life. It is not something that can simply be removed by policy or decree.

This is one of the reasons why His Holiness is seeking a mutually beneficial solution. China has perhaps 250 to 300 million Buddhists, many of whom are searching for spiritual meaning. Tibetans can help preserve and share the Buddhist traditions that have been maintained in Tibet, while Tibetans can benefit from China's economic development. That is the essence of the Middle Way Approach—finding a solution that benefits both sides. Unfortunately, the current Chinese leadership believes Tibet can be controlled through ever greater surveillance and restrictions. Today, these systems are increasingly supported by artificial intelligence and advanced technologies. China is also expanding its influence beyond its borders. We have seen reports of overseas police stations, police cooperation programmes, training initiatives, and the export of surveillance technologies. What is concerning is that these tools and methods are increasingly being shared with other authoritarian governments. In that way, China is not only extending its influence but also strengthening a broader network of like-minded regimes.

Is that what the world wants to see? Is that what India wants to see? Is that what Europe wants to see? If not, then I keep making a simple point. Many of our friends say, "The dragon is biting us." My response is that there was a time when the dragon was weak and poor. Who helped the dragon become so large and powerful? The United States, Europe, Japan, Taiwan, and many others invested in China, traded with China, transferred technology, and helped integrate it into the global economy. Now, if you know the dragon is biting you and you continue to feed it, then the question becomes: whose problem is it?

You are feeding the dragon even more through trade. China is promoting greater market access because it has surplus goods to sell, while the United States is moving in the opposite direction and raising tariffs. These are some of the realities we need to consider when discussing China. We also need to look at how China is engaging with the international community. When the United States steps back from multilateral institutions, China often steps in. Increasingly, it is trying to shape international discussions, including how concepts such as human rights should be interpreted and understood. You can see this across forums such as BRICS and other international platforms. At the same time, there are important developments at the leadership level, with major world leaders engaging China and China seeking a larger role in global affairs. We will have to see how these developments unfold. People often ask how China–India relations affect us. It is not something that can be judged over a short period of time. It is a very dynamic relationship, and we need to watch it carefully over the longer term. Historically, relations have often been one step forward and two steps back. At the moment, there are signs of improvement, partly because China needs access to India's market. But if you look at the trade figures, India's imports from China have grown significantly, while exports have not kept pace. As a result, India continues to run a trade deficit of around US$115 billion annually with China. I am sure the Indian government is concerned about this. At the same time, Indian businesses also need to think carefully about the long-term implications of these trends.

We know that China has a chokehold on rare earth supplies and is increasingly using that leverage to exert influence. Countries seeking access to rare earths often face conditions, including restrictions on how the materials can be used or exported. These measures affect not only India, but also countries in Europe and Southeast Asia. Through this, you can see how China extends its influence beyond its borders. Many people around the world still do not fully appreciate the scale of that influence. That is why we also need to look at the Belt and Road Initiative and its broader implications. In a number of countries, these projects have created significant debt and long-term dependence. At the same time, they have given China a presence in strategically important locations along key maritime and overland trade routes. If you look at places such as Sihanoukville, the Coco Islands, Hambantota, and Gwadar, you can see that these are no longer viewed simply as economic projects. They have acquired strategic significance as well, and that is something the international community needs to pay close attention to.

Several years ago, more than 20 African leaders went to China to renegotiate their loans because they were struggling with repayment. Nothing comes for free. Everything has a price. When countries receive financing for ports, airports, telecommunications, and other major infrastructure projects, there can also be political expectations attached. If countries are expected to align with China's positions on key issues, then the question becomes: how much political autonomy do they really have? Sovereignty is not just about territory or borders. It is also about the ability to make decisions independently and in accordance with your own national interests. Just before coming to this interview, I was reading about the Burmese President's visit to India. Situations like these remind us that dealing with authoritarian governments often involves both moral questions and strategic realities. Balancing those considerations is never easy, especially when it involves neighbouring countries.

Sikyong la, you touched upon the India-China relations here. Historically Tibet has been described as a geopolitical buffer zone between these two nations and especially in today's context of heightened Himalayan border frictions, how would you define Tibet's strategic relevance when it comes to contemporary India-China relations?

I remember Lodi Gyari once saying that Tibet served as a political buffer between India and China for centuries—or, as the Chinese would say, "since time immemorial. "It was only after China occupied Tibet that India and China became direct neighbours. Even today, it is called the Indo-Tibetan Border Police, not the Indo-China Border Police. That reflects an important historical reality. Looking ahead, if there is a peaceful resolution to the Sino-Tibet conflict, Tibet can once again become a bridge between the two most populous countries in the world rather than a source of tension. India, Tibet, and China already share deep historical and cultural connections. Buddhism travelled from India to China and later to Tibet, creating a common civilisational heritage. That is why Tibet has the potential to be not only a geographical bridge, but also a cultural and civilisational bridge between India and China.

Right now, there is a serious trust deficit when it comes to China. If you listen to Chinese leaders today, you may get the impression that China is becoming more responsible on the international stage. But many people remain unconvinced. We have a saying that an elephant has tusks to show and teeth to chew with. In other words, what is said publicly is not always reflected in practice. When China calls for dialogue and negotiated solutions in places such as the Middle East or Ukraine, people naturally ask why the same approach is not applied to Tibetans, East Turkestanis, Mongols, or the people of Hong Kong. That is why many see a double standard between China's international rhetoric and its domestic policies. At the same time, China is very skilled at shaping narratives and presenting its positions in ways that appeal to international audiences. That is why it is important to look not only at what is being said, but also at what is being done.

But that is not the reality of China today. China is increasingly behaving as a hegemonic power, and countries across the region are responding to that reality. From Japan and Taiwan to Australia and Southeast Asia, many countries are increasing defence spending and strengthening their security capabilities. A major reason is China's growing assertiveness, particularly in the South China Sea and other disputed areas .If you look at countries such as Vietnam, they are adapting to what they see as a changing security environment. India is no exception. Today, India is investing more than ever in border infrastructure, defence preparedness, and security. These developments are not happening in isolation. They are part of a broader regional response to China's growing influence and increasingly assertive behaviour.

If you look at these developments across the region, many of them are responses to China's increasingly assertive behaviour, not to India. India has traditionally been a defensive country, focused on protecting its own security and interests. At the same time, India–China relations need to improve. Greater trust has to be built, and that can only happen through sustained engagement and understanding. Historically, Tibet served as a buffer between India and China. In the future, if there is a peaceful resolution to the Sino-Tibet conflict, Tibet can become a bridge between the two countries. Tibetans were once the buffer; today, they want to be the bridge. That is one way greater trust and understanding can develop between India and China.

When you mentioned India and China, we see in the global arena that there are a lot of competing international crises which sort of, according to many, keep the Tibet cause as a growing challenge to be raised in the world arena. So, what are the new diplomatic initiatives and public strategies that your administration intends to deploy to mobilize fresh and even the continued support for the Tibetan movement?

As you mentioned, there are conflicts taking place across the world, and the number seems to be growing. Whether it is 50, 100, or more, what is clear is that we are seeing increasing instability and tension in many regions. In a world that is becoming more uncertain and unpredictable, we also have to ask ourselves: where do we stand, and how do we position ourselves in this changing environment?

The greatest strength of the Tibetan movement is, of course, His Holiness the Dalai Lama. Over the decades, he has built friendships, trust, and goodwill with people across the world and across different political systems. That is why I often say that whatever we have been able to achieve, however modest, is largely because of His Holiness. Even at the age of 90, he continues to meet hundreds of people almost every day. He has always said that as long as he is physically able, he wants to serve humanity. That is also why so many people continue to come to Dharamsala—whether they are ordinary citizens, scholars, former leaders, or political leaders. They come because of the respect they have for His Holiness and the values he represents. Ultimately, much of the strength and success of the Tibetan movement rests on His Holiness' leadership, vision, and lifelong commitment to serving others.

When you look at the amount of violence in the world today, perhaps the Tibetan movement remains one of the few movements still firmly committed to non-violence. We genuinely believe that conflicts can be resolved through dialogue and negotiation rather than through fighting and killing. Ultimately, it is not the identity of the ruler that matters most, but the quality of governance. If people are oppressed, it makes little difference who is in power. If we step back and look at the bigger picture, we are all citizens of the same small world. Borders are created by human beings. From space, you do not see them. Yet conflicts often arise over a few metres of territory, and before long lives are lost .Whether a civilian dies or a soldier dies, it is still a human life. Soldiers also have families, loved ones, and dreams. Their lives are no less valuable. That is why I believe one of the enduring strengths of the Tibetan movement is its foundation in non-violence. At a time when so many conflicts are driven by force, we continue to believe that lasting solutions can only come through peaceful means.

Between 2009 and 2022, around 157 Tibetans self-immolated. Most were young people between the ages of 17 and 35. What is striking is that they never experienced an independent Tibet, nor did they live through the Cultural Revolution. What they witnessed was the reality of Tibet as it exists today. Their intention was not to harm others. They hoped that their sacrifice would draw attention to the suffering of the Tibetan people—either from the Chinese government or, failing that, from the international community. The challenge is that China works very hard to control information and shape narratives. Although we have been able to document and publicise many of these cases, it remains difficult to reach the wider world because of the scale of China's state apparatus .It is not that Tibetans could not have chosen violence. They could have. But His Holiness has always been clear that violence is not the way forward. He has consistently urged Tibetans to follow the path of non-violence, believing that the movement would lose its moral foundation if it embraced violence.

.

On that note, we have seen that the Tibetan movement emanating from two different fronts, mainly the resilient population living inside Tibet, which you mentioned now directly under the Chinese administration, and a highly global Tibetan diaspora led by the Central Tibetan Administration. So, how do you view these distinct yet complementary roles of these two groups and how does your administration continue to bridge the gap between the two?

Tibet is the largest and highest plateau on Earth. Tibetans traditionally call it the Land Surrounded by Snow Mountains, while many in the West know it as the "Roof of the World." Today, environmental scientists often refer to it as the "Third Pole" because it contains the largest concentration of glaciers outside the Arctic and Antarctic. These glaciers feed many of Asia's major rivers, which is why Tibet is also known as the "Water Tower of Asia." Rivers originating in Tibet flow into ten countries and support hundreds of millions of people. Take China itself. The Yellow River and the Yangtze, two of China's most important rivers, both originate on the Tibetan Plateau. The Mekong also begins in Tibet before flowing through Myanmar, Laos, Thailand, Cambodia, and Vietnam. Before it leaves Chinese-controlled territory, China has already built more than 30 dams along its course, giving it significant influence over downstream water flows. The same concerns apply to the Brahmaputra, which originates in Tibet before flowing into India. Near the Great Bend in Medog, China is planning a massive hydropower project that could generate several times more electricity than the Three Gorges Dam. Much of that energy is intended to be transmitted to the industrial centres of eastern and southern China. That is why Tibet is not just a political issue. It is also an environmental, ecological, and water-security issue. What happens on the Tibetan Plateau affects not only Tibetans, but hundreds of millions of people across Asia.

You also have major rivers such as the Indus and the Jhelum that originate in Tibet and flow into India and Pakistan. These rivers have sustained some of the world's oldest civilisations, including the Indus Valley civilisation. Because of its geostrategic location and unique environment, what happens on the Tibetan Plateau has consequences far beyond Tibet itself. I remember coming across studies by climatologists examining the relationship between the "Roof of the World" and the "Lungs of the World"—the Tibetan Plateau and the Amazon rainforest. When I was travelling in Brazil, I read research suggesting links between climatic patterns in these two regions, despite being on opposite sides of the globe. Some studies found that heavier snowfall in Tibet was associated with lower rainfall in the Amazon, while increased rainfall in the Amazon corresponded with reduced snowfall on the Tibetan Plateau. Whether we are talking about water resources, climate systems, biodiversity, or environmental security, Tibet's importance extends far beyond its borders. What happens on the Tibetan Plateau affects not only Asia, but the wider world as well.

We are seeing the effects of environmental and climate change in many parts of the world, from Canada to Europe and beyond. Scientists increasingly point to the interconnected nature of climate systems, including the role of the Tibetan Plateau. As I mentioned earlier, rivers originating in Tibet flow into ten countries and support some of the most densely populated regions on Earth. When control over these river systems changes, the effects are felt far downstream. I have travelled to countries such as Cambodia and Thailand, where people often speak about how changing river flows are affecting their livelihoods. Communities that depend on these rivers for farming, fishing, and daily life are already experiencing the consequences. Some experts estimate that between 1.8 and 2 billion people depend, directly or indirectly, on rivers that originate on the Tibetan Plateau. That gives you a sense of the scale of the issue. So, we are not just talking about environmental concerns. We are talking about future food security, water security, and the livelihoods of millions of people. What happens on the Tibetan Plateau has consequences for the entire region.

When there is excess water, it can be released downstream and contribute to flooding. When there is a shortage, water can be retained upstream, increasing the risk of drought for downstream communities. That is why control over these river systems has become such an important issue. China now controls the source of many of Asia's major rivers, giving it considerable influence over water flows and related resources. For downstream countries, this creates a sense of vulnerability .In the long run, however, shared resources cannot be managed effectively through unilateral control alone. These rivers connect countries and peoples across the region, and their future depends on cooperation and mutual understanding.

Going back to your recent inauguration that took place in 27 May, you made a point to publicly address the rising political polarization happening within the exile community, which is a global trend as it is seen happening globally. How do you plan to foster internal cohesion and would future engagements between yourself and your predecessor, the only two Sikyongs since 2011, take place?

I have not spoken much about divisions within the community because I have always tried to avoid contributing to them. During the previous election, I campaigned mainly online, but I never spoke against any of the other candidates. I had made a personal commitment not to take part in anything that could divide the Tibetan community. Democracy, by its nature, can be divisive because people hold different views and support different candidates. But I have always believed that the unity of the Tibetan people must remain our highest priority. This time, I did not campaign at all. I wanted to remain consistent with that principle. That was the message I was trying to convey.

But anyone who understands the history of the Tibetan exile community would know that these issues are not new. Today, we live in the age of social media. News travels much faster, although not all of it is necessarily accurate or well-informed. That creates its own challenges. If you look back at the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s, there were times when tensions within the community were, in some ways, even greater than they are today. There were fights, stabbings, and disruptions at meetings. These things happened. Many younger Tibetans have not witnessed those experiences firsthand, nor have they always heard about them from the older generation. That is why I often say that disagreements and tensions have existed at different points in our history. What has changed today is not the existence of those differences, but the way they are expressed. The environment has changed, technology has changed, and society has changed. As a result, these issues now take different forms.

But I have always believed that there is a way forward. That is why we follow the Middle Way Approach, which seeks genuine autonomy for Tibet. At the same time, there are Tibetans who continue to advocate for full independence. One reason we place so much emphasis on Tibetan history is that it is important to make clear that history cannot simply be rewritten. Before the People's Republic of China entered Tibet, Tibet was never directly ruled by China. We have historical records and evidence that support this position. China often tells the world that Tibet has been part of China since ancient times. But political statements cannot change historical facts. That is why we continue to study, preserve, and present Tibet's history. We believe the historical record speaks for itself, and when it comes to Tibet's history, we are confident that the evidence is on our side.

The second message is for the international community. We believe countries should not simply repeat China's position on Tibet without examining the historical record for themselves. That is why we encourage policymakers, scholars, and governments to study Tibetan history and look at the evidence before reaching conclusions. We often provide historical materials and ask people to make their own informed judgments based on facts rather than political narratives. When governments automatically accept China's narrative, there is less incentive for Beijing to engage in meaningful dialogue on the Tibet issue. Our position is quite simple. Tibet today is under Chinese rule. That is a fact. Tibet also existed as an independent country in history. That is a fact as well. Any serious discussion about Tibet must take both realities into account.

The third reason is for the younger generation of Tibetans. If we do not study and preserve Tibetan history, then who will? If we lose our history, we risk losing the foundation of our struggle as well. Without understanding our past, it becomes difficult to shape our future. The fourth reason relates to both the Middle Way Approach and those who advocate independence. While our political goals may differ, both sides depend on the same historical foundation. That is why I do not think Tibetans should spend too much time arguing among themselves. We already know our history. The real challenge is to help the international community, the Chinese people, and the Chinese government better understand it. There is still a great deal of education to be done. We often assume that people in India, the United States, and elsewhere understand Tibet. But that is not always the case. Just a few weeks ago, someone from America told me, "I visited the museum, and only then did I realise how large Tibet is. "That illustrates the challenge. Many people still do not fully understand Tibet's size, its strategic importance, its environmental significance, or the value of Tibetan Buddhist culture. That is why our energy should be directed towards education, awareness, and outreach rather than internal disagreements. We need to focus on helping more people understand Tibet and why it matters.

That is what we are trying to do, and I think it has already produced some positive results. Recently, I was conducting a workshop for newly elected members of the Tibetan Parliament-in-Exile. One of the participants, a former president of the Tibetan Youth Congress, told me, "Since you adopted this position, it has made our advocacy work much easier. "Comments like that suggest that people are beginning to see the positive side of the direction we are taking. What is important now is that we bring our energies together and focus on the larger goals before us. After all, we are only about 130,000 to 140,000 Tibetans living in exile. If we spend our energy on internal divisions, we only weaken ourselves. We are already facing a very large and powerful opponent. That is why unity and a shared sense of purpose remain so important. If we can stay focused on the bigger picture, our efforts will be far more effective.

Under your leadership, we have seen global communities around the world celebrate the year of compassion to honour His Holiness the Dalai Lama's 90th birthday. The focus nationally shifts to continuity also. So, beyond celebrity events, how can the global Tibetan community practically institutionalise and preserve the Dalai Lama legacy, his secular philosophy and also pass on what we have learnt to the next generation?

This Year of Compassion is really an opportunity for people to better understand the thinking of His Holiness the Dalai Lama. What is remarkable about His Holiness is that his message is simple and easy to understand. He often says, "I am one among eight billion human beings. As a human being, my responsibility is to promote human values." That is also the spirit behind Social, Emotional and Ethical (SEE) Learning, which I believe is one of His Holiness' most important contributions. The message is straightforward: whether you are a believer or a non-believer, what matters most is that you are a good human being. His Holiness has always encouraged people to practise their own faith while respecting all other religions, as well as those who choose not to follow any religion. That is why SEE Learning is for everyone. It is not limited to Buddhists or religious people. It is meant for believers and non-believers alike because it is ultimately about compassion, ethics, and universal human values.

Then there is His Holiness' role as a Tibetan and his commitment to preserving and promoting ancient Indian wisdom. He often says that many of these traditions of knowledge, philosophy, and ethics have declined in their land of origin, and he has consistently encouraged their revival and wider appreciation. In many ways, that wisdom helped shape His Holiness into the person he is today. That is why he places such importance on sharing it with the world. Whether it is compassion, human values, interfaith harmony, or ancient wisdom, the messages of His Holiness continue to resonate because they speak to something universal in all of us. I believe that anyone who sincerely reflects on these teachings becomes a better person. At the same time, this is not limited to His Holiness alone. Any person—religious or non-religious—who promotes peace, compassion, and understanding deserves our respect. In a world marked by so much division and conflict, voices that bring people together are more important than ever.His Holiness is often regarded as an apostle of peace. People respect him not only because he speaks about peace, but because he lives by what he teaches. In many ways, that is the more difficult part. In the Tibetan tradition, we speak of thab and sherab—method and wisdom. His Holiness has always shown how the two can be brought together in a balanced and harmonious way. Even when faced with difficult questions, he often responds with wisdom, humility, and humour. That is why the message of His Holiness remains so important. I have heard that many of the events organised during the Year of Compassion were very well received. If that is the case, then perhaps these efforts should continue beyond a single year. After all, compassion is not something to be practised only during a Year of Compassion or on a particular day. It has to be practised every day, throughout our lives. That is the message the world needs to understand. The same principle applies to public life. Many politicians describe themselves as religious and regularly visit places of worship. But faith should be reflected in one's actions. Public service is ultimately about serving people, and those entrusted with leadership should place the welfare of the people above personal interests. That is one of the most important lessons His Holiness continues to teach through both his words and his example.

Thank you, Sikongala. On that note, we thank you for sharing your profound insights and for outlining the strategic vision of your administration for the coming five years.

This comprehensive conversation has sort of illuminated the multifaceted challenges and opportunities that the Tibetan communities face and also the global landscape that continues to evolve.

We are deeply honored to have you for this special interview. On behalf of our readers, our editorial team, we thank you for your time, your leadership and your openness. Thank you so much. We are still navigating and trying to stay afloat in this turbulent global politics evolving politics. Thank you for having me.
