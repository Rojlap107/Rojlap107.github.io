Four young Tibetans reflect on exile, identity and what their community gives back to the places that gave it refuge.

Tenzin Woesel

For 67 years, Tibetans have found refuge in India. Yet, if we look closely, we are far more than what a refugee community is traditionally perceived to be. We are active contributors, quietly shaping the social, cultural, and moral fabric of our host nation, Mother India.

In my region, Dharamshala, this contribution is clearly visible. Once a quiet British-era cantonment town, established in 1849, Dharamshala has evolved into a global epicentre of spirituality, dialogue, and cultural exchange following the arrival of His Holiness the Dalai Lama and the Tibetan community. This presence has drawn thousands from across the globe, strengthening the local economy, and positioning Dharamshala as a cornerstone of Himachal Pradesh's tourism.

Moreover, the significance goes beyond the economic or infrastructural growth of Dharamshala. The Tibetan community has played a significant role in India's identity as a diverse and pluralistic society today. In a land that granted us with sanctuary, we have chosen not to live as an outsider, but as engaged individuals.

A Tibetan proverb speaks of 'returning tea for water', a reminder that even the smallest kindness must be repaid with something greater. India gave us hope. In return, we share values of compassion, coexistence, and resilience, striving to turn the bitterness of exile into the sweetness of contribution.

And perhaps one day, when Tibet is free, this bond will endure by evolving from refuge to a lasting partnership rooted in shared history, mutual respect, and a stronger future.

Tenzin Woesel is Regional Coordinator of the Voluntary Tibetan Advocacy Group (VTAG),Dharamshala, dedicated to advancing Tibetan advocacy and youth engagement. Tenzin actively works to raise awareness of Tibetan issues by informing and engaging with youth across the globe.

Tenzin Yangchen

Growing up in Paris, I have seen our Tibetan community evolve from a small group of newcomers into a visible and active part of French society. This journey has been about more than settling down, it's also about how we take part in and contribute to life in France while maintaining and adapting our identity within it.

Many Tibetans have opened small businesses that support local economies while introducing elements of Tibetan culture into daily life. At the same time, more Tibetan youth pursue higher education in prestigious French universities and enter professional fields such as law, business, and social services, including social work. In doing so, they contribute to the same social systems that once welcomed and supported their families.

Culturally, the weekly Lhakar movement brings Tibetans together every Wednesday to dance. This initiative has grown more inclusive, with non-Tibetans participating, turning a community space into a shared cultural experience.

Civically, our engagement can be seen in discussions around the representation of Tibetan heritage at the Guimet Museum, particularly following the reframing of Himalayan collections. This shows how diaspora participation extends into public and institutional dialogue on heritage and representation in France.

Tenzin Yangchen is a member of the Students for a Free Tibet France, Paris.

Tenzin Yanki

Tibetans in France have contributed in various cultural forms, such as hosting Himalayan festivals where locals can discover Tibetan culture. These events often include food stands—where one can enjoy Tibetan cuisine—as well as traditional dance performances. The Circle dance, held every Sunday and Wednesday in public parks, is one such event where locals can enjoy the dance moves and participate in these cultural expressions.

From my perspective, the most impactful contribution is through civic engagement and advocacy. For example, on 30th August 2024 27 scholars published an article (a tribune) in Le Monde titled: "French museums are bowing to China's demands to rewrite history and erase peoples". The article highlighted how two museums in Paris have erased Tibet's name—the Muséd du Quai Branly replaced the term 'Tibet' with 'Xizang', while Musée Guimet relabelled their gallery space from 'Tibet-Nepal' to 'Le monde himalayen' (Himalayan world).

At this critical point, Students for a Free Tibet (SFT) France, along with five other Tibetan associations, launched a campaign through open letters, social media, and demonstrations. On 23rd September 2024, the Muséd du Quai Branly restored the name Tibet. On 21st May 2025, senator Jacqueline Eustache-Brinio raised an issue with the government regarding the Musée Guimet, questioning how a national museum replaced the word 'Tibet' from its gallery space. On 2nd July 2025, SFT France along with five other associations filed an administrative lawsuit for abuse of power; the lawsuit is still ongoing.

These actions demonstrate how the Tibetan community in France has embraced democratic values of the country while restoring its own identity. It also brings awareness among the citizens for transparency and vigilance against foreign interference.

Tenzin Yanki is a member of the Students for a Free Tibet France, Paris.

Kunga Norbu

Tibetan Community Contributions to the Host Country

In the quiet hills of Dharamshala, a unique story of coexistence continues to unfold, one shaped not by policy, but by people. Over the years, the relationship between Tibetan refugees and local Indian communities has grown into a meaningful partnership, rooted in shared spaces, everyday interactions, and a quiet sense of mutual respect.

At the heart of this transformation is H.H the 14th Dalai Lama, whose presence has turned Dharamshala into a global centre for spirituality and learning. The Dalai Lama's temple, along with numerous monasteries, draws visitors from across India and around the world, some seeking answers, others simply a moment of peace. This steady flow of people sustains a vibrant local economy, supporting cafés, guesthouses, handicraft shops, and small businesses run by both Tibetans and Indians.

The depth of this economic connection becomes especially visible during Losar. Markets come alive, streets fill with colour, and business thrives. Yet, when the Dalai Lama once spent Losar in South India, the absence was felt deeply, celebrations grew quieter, and many local shopkeepers experienced a noticeable drop in income, revealing how closely livelihoods are intertwined.

Beyond economics, the cultural and spiritual exchange is equally profound. Tibetan traditions, art, medicine, and philosophy, have enriched the region's diversity, while values like compassion and non-violence quietly influence both locals and visitors. Socially, bonds are built through simple acts: Tibetans renting homes from Indian families, neighbours sharing daily life, and during March 10 Uprising Day gatherings, local residents offering water and refreshments in gestures of solidarity.

Dharamshala today stands not just as a place on the map, but as a living example of harmony, where two communities do more than coexist; they support, shape, and uplift one another in a shared journey of resilience and understanding.
