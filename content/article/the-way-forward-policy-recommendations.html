The Way Forward: Policy Recommendations

Although the contributors approach Tibet from different disciplines and perspectives, several common policy concerns emerge across the articles.

The Editors 1 August 2026 · 4 min read
Share

Although the contributors approach Tibet from different disciplines and perspectives, several common policy concerns emerge across the articles. These recommendations are not intended to reflect the views of every author. Rather, they bring together the principal themes that recur throughout this volume and are organised as short-, medium-, and long-term priorities for both the Government of India and the Central Tibetan Administration (CTA).

Short-Term Priorities

The succession of His Holiness the 14th Dalai Lama is likely to be one of the most significant developments affecting Tibet in the years ahead. India should quietly prepare for the diplomatic, legal, and security implications while maintaining its long-standing position that the recognition of the Dalai Lama is a religious matter to be determined in accordance with Tibetan Buddhist traditions. At the same time, the CTA should continue strengthening its institutional preparedness, preserving key archives, and ensuring close coordination with Tibetan Buddhist communities across the world.

Several contributors also draw attention to the growing importance of information and cyber security. As debates on Tibet increasingly play out in the digital domain, both India and the CTA should strengthen their strategic communications, improve their ability to respond to disinformation, and invest in secure communications and cyber resilience.

Medium-Term Priorities

Many of the issues discussed in this volume ultimately point to the need for stronger institutions and better-informed policymaking.

For India, this means investing more seriously in expertise on Tibet and contemporary China through universities, research institutions, military academies, and diplomatic training. Continued support for Tibetan educational, cultural, and monastic institutions will also help preserve a unique civilisational heritage while reinforcing India's position as the principal home of Tibetan Buddhism in exile.

For the CTA, the priority should be to ensure that younger Tibetans remain connected to their language, culture, and democratic institutions. Education, cultural programmes, scholarships, and greater opportunities for young Tibetans to participate in public life will all contribute to the long-term strength of the exile community.

Long-Term Priorities

Looking further ahead, India would benefit from developing a more coherent and enduring Tibet policy that recognises Tibet's importance beyond the immediate context of India-China relations.

India also has a direct civilisational stake in the future of Tibetan Buddhism. While Buddhism originated in India, Tibet became the principal custodian of the Nalanda tradition after the decline of the great Buddhist universities in India. Through centuries of scholarship, translation and monastic practice, Tibetan Buddhism preserved and developed a rich intellectual and spiritual heritage rooted in the teachings of Nalanda. Attempts to control, reinterpret or undermine this tradition therefore affect not only Tibetan religious and cultural identity but also an important part of India's own civilisational legacy. Preserving the integrity and freedom of Tibetan Buddhism strengthens India's cultural influence, reinforces its role as the home of the living Nalanda tradition, and safeguards a heritage that remains integral to India's civilisational ethos.

The issues discussed throughout this volume—from border security and water resources to Buddhism and regional stability—demonstrate that Tibet will continue to occupy an important place in India's strategic thinking.

India is also uniquely placed to become a global centre for Tibetan studies, Himalayan research, and policy analysis. Building on the institutions and expertise already available would strengthen both scholarship and policymaking.

For the CTA, preparing the next generation of leaders will remain one of its most important responsibilities. Future leaders will operate in a very different political and technological environment from previous generations. Investing in leadership development, governance, diplomacy, and public policy today will help ensure that Tibetan institutions remain effective and resilient in the decades to come.

Conclusion

The articles in this volume suggest that Tibet's future will depend as much on strong institutions, informed leadership, and the preservation of identity as on wider geopolitical developments. Preparing for that future requires sustained investment in knowledge, institutions, and younger generations—an effort that both India and the CTA can begin strengthening today.

At the same time, this volume makes clear that Tibet is far more than a geographical space. Its history, civilisation, and strategic importance extend well beyond the Himalayas, influencing the security, diplomacy, and future of Asia. Understanding Tibet in this broader context is essential not only for informed policymaking, but also for building a more stable and secure region.
