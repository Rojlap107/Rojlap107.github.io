Editor’s Note

The idea of publishing a journal devoted to Tibet and the wider Himalayan region arose from a simple observation.

Srikanth Kondapalli 1 August 2026 · 8 min read
Share

The idea of publishing a journal devoted to Tibet and the wider Himalayan region arose from a simple observation. Although developments in the Himalayas have received increasing attention in recent years, there are still very few publications that examine the region in a comprehensive and interdisciplinary manner. Studies on Tibet are often approached separately through the perspectives of history, religion, security, environment or international relations. While each of these has contributed significantly to our understanding of Tibet, there has been relatively little effort to examine these issues together or to analyse their wider implications for the Himalayan region and Asia.

Over the years, scholarship on Tibet in India has remained dispersed across different institutions and disciplines. There is still no sustained platform that brings together strategic studies, history, political science, environmental studies, Buddhism, law and regional perspectives within a single publication devoted to Tibet and the Himalayan region. Trans Himalaya is intended to contribute to that larger effort. We hope it will encourage new scholarship, support younger researchers and strengthen the study of Tibet within India's academic and policy communities.

Trans Himalaya has been conceived with this objective in mind. It seeks to provide a forum where different disciplines, perspectives and experiences can engage with one another. The intention is not to replace specialised scholarship but to complement it by encouraging dialogue across disciplines and by placing contemporary developments within a broader historical and strategic context.

It is therefore appropriate that the inaugural issue is devoted to Tibet.

For India, Tibet is not merely a neighbouring region beyond the Himalayas. It occupies an important place in India's strategic environment, civilisational history and cultural memory. The two share centuries-old connections through Buddhism, trade, scholarship and pilgrimage. At the same time, developments in Tibet today have acquired increasing relevance for India's security, border management, water resources, environmental concerns and relations with China. Any serious assessment of the Himalayan region must therefore take account of developments on the Tibetan Plateau.

This issue proceeds from the view that Tibet cannot be understood through any single discipline. Historical experience remains important, but it does not by itself explain contemporary realities. Similarly, security concerns cannot be examined in isolation from questions relating to ecology, infrastructure, governance, religion, technology or the changing social and economic conditions within Tibet. These developments increasingly intersect and together shape the strategic environment of the Himalayas.

The contributions brought together in this volume reflect this broader approach. They include perspectives from historians, diplomats, strategic analysts, environmental specialists, Tibetan scholars, younger researchers and Chinese perspectives. Some essays revisit historical developments and enduring questions relating to Tibet's political status and civilisation. Others examine contemporary developments within Tibet, India's evolving approach towards Tibet, regional perspectives, Buddhism and the wider geopolitical changes taking place across Asia. Considered together, they underline a simple point: Tibet is neither only a historical issue nor solely a bilateral question between India and China. Its significance extends much further, touching upon a range of political, strategic, environmental and cultural issues that will continue to shape the Himalayan region in the years ahead.

One of the distinguishing features of Trans Himalaya is its emphasis on primary-source research. Understanding developments in Tibet today requires a careful study of Chinese policies and institutions. It also requires engagement with Chinese-language publications, official documents, laws, policy papers and debates taking place within academic and policy circles in the People's Republic of China. These sources are indispensable for understanding how the Chinese leadership views Tibet, the objectives it seeks to pursue, and the methods through which those objectives are implemented. At the same time, they need to be examined critically and alongside other scholarly sources. Examining these materials is not an endorsement of official Chinese narratives; rather, it is essential for developing a more informed and evidence-based understanding of contemporary developments.

The journal also seeks to contribute to a stronger Indian discourse on Tibet and the Himalayan region. India's relationship with Tibet has evolved over centuries through religion, commerce, scholarship and geography. Today, developments in Tibet have a direct bearing on India's security environment, border management, water security and wider strategic interests. Despite this, sustained research on Tibet from an Indian perspective has remained relatively limited. It is our hope that Trans Himalaya will contribute to a more informed discussion by providing a forum where scholarship, policy analysis and informed debate can interact.

An important objective of the journal is to encourage greater engagement between research and public policy. Academic work often remains confined to universities and specialised institutions, while policy discussions do not always benefit from the findings of current research. We believe that there is value in bringing these two closer together. Each issue will therefore include a section presenting policy recommendations arising from the themes discussed in the volume. These recommendations are intended to encourage discussion rather than prescribe a particular course of action. They are offered in the hope that they will be useful to the Government of India, the Central Tibetan Administration and others engaged with issues relating to Tibet and the Himalayan region.

The journal will also include a recurring section that looks beyond immediate developments. Public policy is necessarily concerned not only with present realities but also with the challenges that may emerge in the years ahead. Each issue will therefore carry an essay examining longer-term trends, possible scenarios and their implications. The purpose is not to predict the future with certainty, but to encourage strategic thinking on questions that are likely to shape the Himalayan region in the coming years.

In preparing this volume, we have also sought to make it useful as a reference work. Alongside the essays, readers will find maps, timelines, photographs, institutional profiles and other reference material intended to provide context for the issues discussed. These materials are included with the hope that they will be of value to students, researchers, policymakers and general readers alike.

Although the inaugural issue is devoted to Tibet, the scope of Trans Himalaya is considerably wider. Future issues will examine other parts of Asia's uplands, including Arunachal Pradesh, Bhutan, Nepal and Mongolia, as well as subjects such as Buddhism, borderland societies, environmental change, technology, infrastructure and regional security. The intention is to encourage informed scholarship on a region that is undergoing profound political, economic and strategic transformation.

India's understanding of Tibet cannot remain episodic. It requires sustained research, informed public debate and greater institutional attention. We hope this journal will make a modest contribution in that direction.

In preparing this inaugural issue, we have also sought to make it useful as a reference volume. Alongside the essays, readers will find historical timelines, maps, institutional overviews, photographs and other reference material. These have been included to provide context and to assist readers who may wish to pursue particular subjects in greater detail. It is our intention that every issue of Trans Himalaya should serve not only as a collection of scholarly articles but also as a practical resource for researchers, students, policymakers and others interested in the Himalayan region.

Although this inaugural issue is devoted entirely to Tibet, the journal itself has a much wider scope. Future editions will examine developments across the Trans-Himalayan region, including Arunachal Pradesh, Bhutan, Nepal and Mongolia, as well as issues relating to Buddhism, borderlands, technology, environmental change and regional security. The Himalayas are undergoing rapid political, economic and strategic transformation. We hope the journal will provide a platform for informed scholarship on these developments and encourage greater dialogue among scholars, practitioners and policymakers.

This journal has benefited from the encouragement and support of many individuals and institutions. I am deeply grateful to Ambassador Shyam Saran for readily agreeing to contribute the Foreword despite his many commitments. His reflections place Tibet within the broader context of India's strategic interests and civilisational engagement with the Himalayan region, and provide an appropriate introduction to this inaugural volume.

I am equally grateful to all the contributors who accepted the invitation to write for the first issue. At the time the journal was conceived, Trans Himalaya existed only as an idea. Their willingness to support the project from the outset made it possible to bring this inaugural issue to fruition. The diversity of their scholarship and experience has enriched the volume in ways that would not otherwise have been possible.

I also wish to acknowledge my colleagues at the Foundation for Non-violent Alternatives, who have worked tirelessly over many months to transform an idea into a publication. I am indebted to the members of the Governing Council for their guidance and encouragement throughout this process. Ms Sona Kapila devoted considerable care and patience to the language editing of the manuscript. I also record my appreciation to Academic Foundation for their professionalism and commitment in publishing this volume. Finally, I acknowledge with gratitude the donor whose confidence and support made the publication of this inaugural issue possible.

The success of a journal cannot be measured by its first issue alone. It depends upon its ability to sustain high standards of scholarship, encourage informed debate and remain relevant to changing circumstances. We hope that Trans Himalaya will contribute to a better understanding of Tibet and the wider Himalayan region, encourage serious research, and promote informed discussion on issues of strategic importance. If, over time, it helps strengthen scholarship on the Himalayas and contributes to better-informed public policy, it will have fulfilled the purpose for which it was established.
