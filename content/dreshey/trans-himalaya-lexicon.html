Trans Himalaya Lexicon

Key people, places, institutions and concepts appearing throughout this inaugural issue — concise explanations for readers from diverse disciplinary backgrounds.

Administrative & Political Geography

Tibet
Lies at the centre of Asia, spanning 2.5 million square kilometres — by landmass, it would rank among the world’s ten largest countries had it not been occupied by the PRC in 1950. Encircled by the Earth’s highest mountains, this vast, arid plateau, with an average altitude of 14,000 feet, is home to unique ecosystems and the source of Asia’s major rivers, supplying water to 85% of Asia’s population. Historically home to the Tibetan people and defined by a distinct civilisation, language, religion and political history, Tibet comprised three traditional provinces — Ü-Tsang, Kham and Amdo — with Lhasa as its political, administrative and spiritual centre.
Amdo
The north-eastern region of historic Tibet, one of its three traditional provinces alongside Kham and Ü-Tsang, and long an important centre of Tibetan religious scholarship and cultural life, producing figures including His Holiness the 14th Dalai Lama. Much of historic Amdo today falls within Qinghai Province, with other Tibetan-populated areas administered under Gansu and Sichuan.
Kham
One of the three traditional provinces of historic Tibet, in the east, characterised by rugged mountains, deep river valleys and strong regional identities. Kham occupied an important position in Tibetan trade networks and became known for its role in resistance movements during periods of political upheaval.
Ü-Tsang
The central and western regions of historic Tibet, traditionally regarded as its political and religious core. It includes Lhasa, Tibet’s historic capital, Shigatse, and many of its most important monasteries and centres of learning, and served as the seat of the Gaden Phodrang Government and the Dalai Lama Institution.
Tibet Autonomous Region (TAR)
An administrative region established by the PRC in 1965, covering only part of historic Tibet — primarily central and western Tibet. Large Tibetan-populated areas of Amdo and Kham were instead incorporated into the neighbouring provinces of Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu and Yunnan, so the TAR represents only a portion of historic Tibet.
East Turkestan
The historic homeland of the Uyghur people and other Turkic Muslim communities of Central Asia. Following the establishment of the PRC in 1949, the region came under CCP control despite earlier movements for self-rule, and is today administered as the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region (XUAR) — the focus of sustained international concern over mass detention, surveillance and coercive labour.
Southern Mongolia
The historic homeland of the Southern Mongols, administered since 1949 as the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. “Southern Mongolia” is preferred by many Southern Mongols, scholars and advocacy organisations as reflecting the region’s historical identity rather than its administrative designation, amid concerns over Mongolian-language education, demographic change and pastoral livelihoods.

Race and Identities

The indigenous peoples whose histories, identities and experiences are central to the themes explored in this volume.

Tibetans
The indigenous people of the Tibetan Plateau and the bearers of one of Asia’s oldest and most distinctive civilisations, whose history, religion, language and cultural traditions are central to understanding Tibet’s past and present.
Uyghurs
A predominantly Turkic Muslim people indigenous to East Turkestan, whose language, culture and history have evolved for centuries at the crossroads of Central Asian civilisation.
Southern Mongols
The indigenous Mongolian people of Southern Mongolia, possessing a distinct linguistic, cultural and pastoral heritage and an important role in the history of Inner Asia.

Government & Institutions — Tibetan

Central Tibetan Administration (CTA)
Established on 29 April 1959 by His Holiness the 14th Dalai Lama in Mussoorie, Uttarakhand, following his arrival in India, and now headquartered in Dharamshala. The CTA functions as the Tibetan Government-in-Exile and the principal representative institution of Tibetans inside Tibet and across the diaspora, responsible for democratic governance, education, religion, cultural preservation, healthcare, refugee welfare and international outreach.
Gaden Phodrang
Established in 1518, the traditional government of Tibet under the Dalai Lama Institution from the seventeenth century, uniting religious and temporal authority in Lhasa until 1959. The institution continued in exile until 2011, when the 14th Dalai Lama formally devolved his political authority to Tibet’s democratically elected leadership.
Kashag
The cabinet, or governing council, of Tibet under the Gaden Phodrang system, comprising monastic and lay officials who administered political, judicial and financial affairs on behalf of the Dalai Lama. Today the Kashag continues as the executive branch of the CTA, headed by the Sikyong.
Sikyong
The democratically elected political leader of the CTA. The title — “Political Leader” or “Protector of the State” — formally replaced Kalon Tripa in 2012 following the Dalai Lama’s devolution of political authority, and was rendered in English as “President” from 2017.
Charter of the Tibetans in Exile
The constitutional document governing the CTA and the exile community, adopted in 1991. It sets out the executive, legislative and judicial institutions of the CTA, defines the rights and responsibilities of Tibetans in exile, and provides the legal basis for the Sikyong, the Tibetan Parliament-in-Exile and the CTA’s other institutions.
Middle Way Approach
The policy framework proposed by the 14th Dalai Lama seeking genuine autonomy for Tibet within the PRC, rather than full independence, through peaceful dialogue and non-violence — and remains central to the CTA’s diplomatic position.
Westphalian State System
The international order emerging from the 1648 Peace of Westphalia, under which sovereign states exercise exclusive authority over a defined territory and derive legitimacy from effective control over it. Tibet presents a distinctive exception: although the CTA exercises no territorial jurisdiction, it functions as the democratically elected representative institution of Tibetans inside Tibet and in exile — an important case study in legitimacy, representation and governments-in-exile.

Religion, Philosophy & Institutions

Nalanda Tradition
The classical Buddhist scholastic tradition developed at Nalanda Mahavihara and other great monastic universities of ancient India between the fifth and twelfth centuries CE, characterised by rigorous philosophical inquiry, logic and debate. Preserved and developed by Tibetan scholars after Buddhism’s decline in India, it remains the philosophical foundation of Tibetan Buddhism and a shared civilisational heritage linking India and Tibet.
Tibetan Buddhism
A form of Vajrayana Buddhism developed through the integration of Indian Buddhist philosophy, tantric traditions and Tibetan culture, encompassing the four principal schools of Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya and Gelug — a defining pillar of Tibetan civilisation shaping society, identity, education, art and governance.
Succession (in Tibetan Buddhism)
The process by which spiritual leadership continues after the death of an important lama, particularly a reincarnate lama (tulku) such as the Dalai Lama or Panchen Lama, through the interpretation of religious signs, consultation with senior monks and oracles, and the identification of a child recognised as the reincarnation — a matter of both deep religious and considerable political significance.
Avalokiteshvara (Chenrezig)
The Bodhisattva of Compassion, one of the most venerated figures in Mahayana Buddhism. The Dalai Lamas are traditionally regarded as manifestations of Chenrezig, underpinning the spiritual authority and historical role of the Dalai Lama institution.
Tulku
A reincarnated lama or spiritual master recognised according to established religious traditions and spiritual signs, providing continuity of spiritual leadership across generations and central today to debates over the recognition and succession of senior religious leaders, most notably the Dalai Lama.
Gaden Phodrang Trust
Established in 2011 by the 14th Dalai Lama, a non-profit religious body overseeing his spiritual and institutional legacy. It has acquired geopolitical importance through the Dalai Lama’s declaration that it holds sole legitimate authority over his future reincarnation — directly challenging Chinese state attempts to control the succession.
Dalai Lama Institution
The spiritual leadership institution of Tibetan Buddhism, historically combining religious and temporal authority. Traditionally regarded as manifestations of Chenrezig, the Dalai Lamas have played a central role in Tibet’s cultural identity, governance, diplomacy and international engagement.
Panchen Lama
One of the highest-ranking figures in Tibetan Buddhism, historically important to the recognition of the Dalai Lama’s reincarnation. The institution became the focus of international attention after the 1995 disappearance of Gedhun Choekyi Nyima, recognised by the 14th Dalai Lama as the 11th Panchen Lama, after which Beijing appointed a separate, state-approved Panchen Lama.
Khalkha Jetsun Dhampa
The highest-ranking reincarnate lama in Mongolia and spiritual leader of its Gelug tradition, a lineage established in the seventeenth century with close ties to Tibet. In 2023, the 14th Dalai Lama publicly recognised the 10th Khalkha Jetsun Dhampa, reaffirming the historic religious relationship between Tibet and Mongolia.

Government & Institutions — PRC

People’s Republic of China (PRC)
The sovereign state established on 1 October 1949 following the Chinese Communist Party’s victory in the civil war. Governed under a one-party system, it administers the Tibet Autonomous Region and Tibetan areas within Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu and Yunnan.
Chinese Communist Party (CCP)
The ruling political party of the PRC, founded in 1921, exercising leadership over the state, military and major public institutions. Through organs such as the United Front Work Department, it formulates policy on ethnic affairs, religion, governance and security in Tibet.
United Front Work Department
A CCP organ responsible for influence operations, elite co-option, diaspora engagement, religious management and political outreach — playing a central role in overseeing religious institutions and shaping official narratives on Tibetan Buddhism and national identity.

Chinese Policies & Governance

Sinicisation
Policies aimed at reshaping non-Han ethnic and religious communities in line with CCP ideology and Han cultural norms, in Tibet affecting language use, monastery administration, education and urbanisation.
Patriotic Education
Political indoctrination campaigns conducted in monasteries, schools and other institutions, emphasising loyalty to the CCP, opposition to “separatism” and acceptance of official narratives on national identity and territorial integrity.
Civil-Military Fusion
A PRC strategic policy integrating civilian technological, industrial and infrastructure development with military modernisation. Along the Himalayan frontier this is reflected in dual-use roads, railways, airports and border villages supporting both civilian activity and military operations.
Ecological Civilisation
A CCP political and environmental concept promoted as the guiding framework for sustainable development, whose implementation in Tibet critics argue has been used to justify state-led relocation programmes, resource extraction and hydropower development.
One China Principle
Beijing’s official policy that there is only one China, that Taiwan is inseparable from it, and that the PRC government is the sole legitimate government of China. Since the 1970s Beijing has increasingly sought to extend the principle’s application from Taiwan to Tibet and other territories.
Xizang
The official Chinese-language term (西藏) for what the PRC administers as the Tibet Autonomous Region, increasingly promoted in English-language publications and diplomacy in place of “Tibet.” Tibetan organisations and many scholars argue this seeks to narrow the concept of Tibet to the TAR’s administrative boundaries, diminishing the broader historical region of Ü-Tsang, Kham and Amdo.

Economics, Governance & Development

Political Economy
The study of how political institutions, state policies and power relationships shape economic systems and resource allocation. Applied to Tibet, it examines how state-led development, resource extraction and fiscal policy have reshaped the region’s economy.
Disempowered Development
A model of development driven primarily by state investment and external actors, in which local populations have limited influence over decisions affecting their land, livelihoods and resources — used to analyse the relationship between state-led growth and the changing economic position of Tibetan communities.
Transfer Payments
Funds transferred from the central government to regional governments, institutions or individuals without a corresponding exchange of goods or services. In Tibet, these have financed development and public services while raising questions of fiscal dependency and local economic autonomy.

Strategic Resources & Geopolitics

Critical Minerals
Mineral resources vital to economic and technological development and national security, including rare earths, lithium, cobalt and copper. Substantial deposits identified across the Tibetan Plateau have heightened its strategic importance while raising concerns over water security and the impact of large-scale mining on fragile high-altitude ecosystems.

Security & Strategy

Sino-Indian Boundary Question
The unresolved territorial dispute between India and China along the Himalayan frontier, spanning the western, middle and eastern sectors including Aksai Chin and Arunachal Pradesh, and closely linked to China’s entry into Tibet in the 1950s.
Buffer State
A political entity positioned between major competing powers to reduce direct confrontation. Tibet historically separated British India from Tsarist Russia and, later, the PRC; the erosion of its autonomy in the 1950s replaced this buffer with a direct India–China frontier.
Grey Zone Warfare
Coercive activities that fall below the threshold of conventional armed conflict but alter strategic realities incrementally — along the Himalayan frontier, including cyber operations, disinformation, border incursions and infrastructure expansion.

Treaties, Borders & Historical Agreements

Simla Convention (1914)
A tripartite conference between Britain, Tibet and China held in Shimla from 1913–14 to discuss Tibet’s status and boundaries. Britain and Tibet initiated and accepted the Convention and its boundary arrangements; China ultimately refused to ratify it.
McMahon Line
The boundary negotiated between British India and Tibet during the 1914 Simla Conference by Sir Henry McMahon and Tibetan representatives. India regards it as the legal boundary in the eastern sector of the Himalayan frontier; China disputes its legitimacy.
17-Point Agreement
Formally the “Agreement of the Central People’s Government and the Local Government of Tibet on Measures for the Peaceful Liberation of Tibet,” signed in Beijing on 23 May 1951. It recognised Chinese sovereignty over Tibet while promising to preserve Tibet’s existing political system and the Dalai Lama’s authority. The Tibetan Government-in-Exile has consistently maintained it was signed under military coercion, without full powers or the Tibetan national seal, and formally repudiated it after the 1959 uprising on the grounds that it had been imposed under duress and repeatedly violated.
